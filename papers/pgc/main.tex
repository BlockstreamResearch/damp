    \begin{abstract}
        We describe and implement a confidential audit construction for regulated assets on Liquid.
        Each regulated output carries a proof that its native Elements value commitment and an issuer audit handle use the same blinding factor.
        The proof is verified inside the Simplicity spending path and is bound to the transaction, output index, asset, deployment, audit epoch, output script, and recovery bytes.
        Holders transfer assets without issuer participation.
        The issuer can recover amounts from authenticated auxiliary data or attempt a bounded discrete-log fallback, while other observers retain Liquid's ordinary amount confidentiality.
        A bounded indexer verifies covenant witnesses, follows policy transitions, reconciles supply, and signs the exact report bytes with an issuer-certified report key.
        A fresh Liquid testnet run exercised bootstrap, two autonomous transfers, reissuance, exact-output blocking, blocked-spend rejection, audited distribution, and a complete $1100=1100$ report.
        This is implementation and testnet evidence, not a proof of production security or provider-independent chain completeness.
    \end{abstract}

    \section{Introduction}

    Liquid confidential transactions hide asset amounts from public observers while preserving ledger balance rules \cite{liquid}.
    Regulated assets may also need selective audit and a policy that restricts future spending.
    DAMP introduced a Simplicity covenant model for such assets \cite{damp-paper}.
    Its first generation exposed regulated amounts and enforced policy.
    The second generation adds confidential holder transfers with covenant-checked issuer recovery.

    The construction here is the second protocol generation.
    It borrows the audit goal and twisted-ElGamal intuition of PGC \cite{pgc}, but it does not transplant PGC's account system or its equal-value proof.
    Instead, it proves a relation over the value commitment already present in an Elements output.
    This matters because the covenant checks the actual ledger commitment rather than a host-supplied surrogate.

    The design has three boundaries.
    First, the onchain Simplicity program verifies a native commitment proof for every regulated output and enforces the active exact-output policy \cite{simplicity}.
    Second, issuer-only recovery runs offchain and does not participate in holder authorization.
    Third, a report service indexes a bounded chain interval and signs its result.
    The signature authenticates the report and its issuer authorization, but does not turn one chain provider into independent evidence.

    We state the construction, its covenant and recovery rules, the reporting model, and measured evidence.
    Claims are separated into algebraic arguments, implementation invariants, observed testnet results, and explicit limits.

    \section{Protocol model}
    \label{sec:model}

    \subsection{Roles and ledger objects}

    Let $\mathcal{G}$ be the prime-order secp256k1 group of order $q$.
    Let $G$ be its standard generator and $H_A$ the Elements generator for regulated asset $A$.
    A confidential output value commitment is
    \begin{equation}
        C=vH_A+bG,
        \label{eq:commitment}
    \end{equation}
    for amount $v$ and nonzero value blinder $b$.

    The issuer selects an audit secret $s$ and publishes
    \begin{equation}
        P=sG.
        \label{eq:audit-key}
    \end{equation}
    A sender adds the audit handle
    \begin{equation}
        D=bP.
        \label{eq:handle}
    \end{equation}
    The issuer computes
    \begin{equation}
        C-s^{-1}D=vH_A.
        \label{eq:recovery-target}
    \end{equation}
    Recovering $v$ from this point is a discrete-log search over an application range.

    Holders authorize ordinary transfers with their own keys.
    The issuer is not a co-signer and need not be online.
    The issuer key signs both policy changes and reissuance through the covenant's governance branch.
    A report key signs audit reports only after an issuer key certifies that report key for one deployment.
    Holder, issuer, audit, and report keys use distinct derivation roles, but the current software derives them from one signer mnemonic.

    \subsection{Deployment and policy state}

    A deployment fixes the network, regulated asset, genesis anchor, issuer key, audit key and epoch, and the compiled protocol profile.
    The active policy commits to a set of exact transaction outpoints.
    A listed outpoint cannot take the ordinary holder-transfer path.
    The policy does not describe a person or an address, and it does not block other outputs controlled by the same wallet.

    Policy succession is an issuer-signed covenant state transition.
    Each successor names the parent policy root and parent verifier-script hash, increments the sequence, and installs a new verifier script.
    The covenant also admits issuer reissuance-to-self under the unchanged policy.
    Other governance-shaped movement is unexpected and causes audit reporting to stop short of completeness.

    \section{Native audit construction}
    \label{sec:construction}

    \subsection{Statement and proof}

    For each regulated output, the public statement contains
    \begin{equation}
        x=(d,g,e,P,h,i,A,C,S,\sha(R)),
        \label{eq:statement}
    \end{equation}
    where $d$ is the deployment identifier, $g$ the protocol generation, $e$ the audit epoch, $h$ the transaction's \texttt{sig\_all\_hash}, $i$ the output index, $A$ the regulated asset, $C$ the native value commitment, $S$ the hash of the output script, and $R$ the fixed-size auxiliary recovery record.
    The witness is $(v,b)$ satisfying Equations~\ref{eq:commitment} and \ref{eq:handle}.

    The prover samples $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbf{Z}_q$ and publishes
    \begin{align}
        T_C &= \alpha H_A+\beta G, & T_D &= \beta P.
    \end{align}
    It derives the challenge $c$ by BIP-340-style tagged SHA-256 over the complete statement, $D$, $T_C$, and $T_D$, reduced modulo $q$ \cite{bip340}.
    The responses are
    \begin{align}
        z_v&=\alpha+cv\pmod q, & z_b&=\beta+cb\pmod q.
    \end{align}
    The verifier checks
    \begin{align}
        z_vH_A+z_bG &= T_C+cC, \label{eq:verify-commitment}\\
        z_bP &= T_D+cD. \label{eq:verify-handle}
    \end{align}

    Honest proofs satisfy both equations by substitution.
    Two accepting transcripts with the same commitments and distinct challenges extract $v$ and $b$ by the standard Sigma-protocol difference equations.
    The noninteractive implementation uses Fiat--Shamir, so its knowledge claim is in the random-oracle model and depends on the usual transform assumptions \cite{faust-fiat-shamir}.
    This algebraic argument has not received an independent cryptographic audit.

    \subsection{Binding to the spend}

    The covenant reconstructs the statement from transaction data and its compiled deployment parameters.
    Binding to \texttt{sig\_all\_hash} commits the proof to the finalized transaction.
    The explicit output index prevents moving a record between outputs.
    Asset, commitment, script hash, deployment, generation, and epoch prevent reuse across assets, destinations, protocol versions, deployments, or audit-key epochs.
    Hashing the recovery bytes prevents an intermediary from replacing the issuer's recovery record while retaining the proof.

    Recovery code does not accept these fields from an unauthenticated description.
    It re-executes the actual covenant witness, extracts one canonical audit-record witness, checks the native range-proof profile, rebuilds the statement from the transaction, and verifies Equations~\ref{eq:verify-commitment} and \ref{eq:verify-handle}.
    Chain inclusion remains the indexer's job.

    The implementation supports at most ten regulated inputs and ten regulated outputs per transfer.
    Audit records use 4,096 Simplicity budget words.
    Executable tests check that the audited transaction shapes fit their consensus execution budgets \cite{pgc-lifecycle}.
    These limits are operational constraints, not security arguments.

    \subsection{Amount interval}

    Application construction accepts amounts from $1$ through $2^{63}-1$ base units.
    The covenant accepts the endpoint $2^{63}$ because the pinned native range-proof header and commitment binding establish that interval; the Sigma relation alone does not establish a range.
    Recovery reports this endpoint truthfully as outside the application cap.
    Supply arithmetic uses wider integers and JSON encodes amounts as decimal strings, so aggregate reports do not wrap at signed 64-bit limits.

    \section{Recovery and confidentiality}
    \label{sec:recovery}

    \subsection{Authenticated fast path}

    The sender encrypts $(v,b)$ to the audit public key with ephemeral ECDH and AES-256-GCM-SIV.
    Associated data commits to the deployment, epoch, output index, asset, native commitment, and output script.
    Successful decryption is not enough: recovery recomputes $C$ and $D$ and verifies the native proof before presenting the amount.

    The recovery ciphertext is a fast path, not a transfer-validity condition.
    A missing, unsupported, or unauthenticated record does not veto a transfer whose native proof and covenant witness are valid.
    This avoids giving the issuer an online liveness role.
    It also means complete recovery is not guaranteed when a sender withholds valid auxiliary data.

    \subsection{Bounded fallback}

    If the fast path fails, the issuer can use Equation~\ref{eq:recovery-target} and a baby-step giant-step search.
    The native library caps one request at $2^{32}$ and bounds its table to about 65,537 public entries.
    The HTTP service uses a smaller $2^{20}$ cap.
    If the selected interval is exhausted, the output amount is unknown.
    The implementation never relabels exhaustion as zero.
    An unresolved unspent amount prevents report completeness; a spent historical row does not enter the current unspent total.

    \subsection{What remains confidential}

    Public observers see the ordinary Liquid transaction fields, the native commitment, the audit handle, the proof, and the recovery bytes.
    The issuer can recover an amount because it knows $s$ or can decrypt the auxiliary record.
    For parties without that secret, amount privacy is computational.
    It relies on the hiding of Elements commitments, DDH-type hardness for the linked handle, honest-verifier zero knowledge of the underlying Sigma relation and the Fiat--Shamir model, plus ECDH and authenticated-encryption security for the recovery record \cite{pgc,faust-fiat-shamir}.
    We do not supply a new reduction that composes these assumptions.

    This paper does not prove universal payment confidentiality.
    Transaction structure, fees, scripts, timing, input and output counts, and queried transaction identifiers remain observable.
    Public Esplora reporting reveals queried identifiers to the provider.
    A private Elements node is required when that access pattern is sensitive.

    \section{Bounded reporting}
    \label{sec:reporting}

    The report service scans linked blocks from bootstrap through a confirmation-bounded snapshot.
    The service used for the fresh lifecycle experiment followed at most 256 blocks, 10,000 transactions, and 512 anchor transitions.
    A request supplies at most 128 authenticated policies.
    The transaction cache is capped at 32 MiB or 1,024 entries.
    A subsequent working-tree revision replaces those total-history limits with resumable public indexing in SQLite and bounded batches.
    Deterministic fixtures cover 350 blocks, 11,200 transactions, and 600 anchor transitions.
    After full local Liquid testnet synchronization, a fresh report covered 349 blocks and 418 transactions from bootstrap through confirmed height 2,608,703, with both signatures verified, matching issued and unspent totals of 1,100, and zero unresolved outputs.
    The larger transaction and transition counts remain fixture evidence.
    This revision retains explicit request, report-size, storage, and recovery bounds and does not persist decrypted reports.

    For each candidate transaction, the service decodes public issuance and output data, identifies the current covenant anchor, re-executes the witness, and recovers regulated output amounts.
    It cross-checks live output status where the provider's snapshot semantics allow it.
    It then computes issued, known-unspent, burned, and unresolved totals.
    Conservation is reported only over the amounts established by those checks.

    Completeness fails closed for every condition represented in a report.
    Unknown policy successors, off-anchor issuance or value, unexpected governance, unavailable amounts, outspend disagreement, or conservation mismatch produce an incomplete report.
    Missing transaction data, covenant execution errors, resource-budget exhaustion, and detected reorganizations abort the request instead of returning \texttt{complete: true}.
    Governance can deliberately terminate coverage, so auditability is not an irrevocable ledger property.

    The service signs canonical report JSON with a report key.
    A separate issuer signature certifies that key for one deployment, network, issuer key, and audit key.
    The UI verifies the certificate first, then verifies the exact report bytes, and finally checks the deployment and network fields before rendering.
    These signatures establish origin and byte integrity.
    They do not prove that the configured chain provider was honest, complete, or independent.

    \section{Implementation evidence}
    \label{sec:evidence}

    \subsection{Local and adversarial tests}

    Rust tests cover proof construction, mutation rejection, authenticated recovery, missing and invalid auxiliary data, bounded-search success and exhaustion, the native $2^{63}$ endpoint, witness parsing, policy transitions, and report signing \cite{pgc-implementation}.
    The signer suite executes confidential transfers at all three supported tree depths, including ten-input and ten-output transactions, and checks issuer, holder, policy-proof and witness-budget rejection \cite{pgc-lifecycle}.
    Native audit unit tests cover missing or invalid auxiliary data alongside a valid proof, rejection of mutated proof fields, and bounded-search exhaustion that preserves an unknown amount.

    The web suite checks schema boundaries, canonical deployment and policy validation, predecessor-policy resolution, localhost report-endpoint restrictions, and the signed-JSON download path.
    Signer tests cover report and certificate signature verification and mutation rejection.
    These tests establish implemented behavior for the exercised cases.
    They do not show that all malformed transactions reject or that the system is production ready.

    \section{Security considerations}
    \label{sec:security}

    The main safety property is narrow.
    A successful holder spend under the pinned covenant has a native audit proof for each regulated output, tied to the transaction and active policy.
    Given the audit secret, Equation~\ref{eq:recovery-target} removes the value blinder.
    Efficient amount recovery still depends on valid auxiliary data or a tractable search interval.

    The exact-output policy limits future spending of listed outputs.
    It does not undo confirmed transactions, prove misconduct, identify a recipient, infer intent, or create an owner-wide freeze.
    Invalid recovery bytes are evidence about submitted bytes only.

    Report completeness depends on one configured provider and a bounded scan.
    Block-link and outspend cross-checks against the same provider are useful consistency checks, not independent confirmation or an SPV proof.
    Reorganizations after the reported snapshot can change ledger state.

    Separate derivation paths limit accidental cross-use, but one signer mnemonic remains the common root for holder, issuer, audit, and report keys.
    Compromise of that mnemonic compromises all derived roles.
    The audit-service credential export contains audit and report secrets plus issuer-recorded openings; it does not contain the issuer or holder signing secret.
    Production use needs a sharper key-custody design, independent cryptographic and implementation review, side-channel analysis, longer-lived indexing, provider diversity, and operational recovery procedures.

    The test evidence is one successful network execution plus bounded local and regtest cases.
    It does not prove confidentiality for every environment, complete recovery against malicious senders, absence of inflation outside the scanned state, or freedom from protocol defects.

    \section{Conclusion}

    The second DAMP generation connects selective audit to the native Liquid value commitment and enforces that relation in the Simplicity spending path.
    Holders retain autonomous transfer authorization, while the issuer receives an authenticated recovery path and a bounded algebraic fallback.
    Exact-output policy and fail-closed reporting keep governance and audit conclusions narrow.

    Fresh Liquid testnet evidence shows the intended lifecycle and a complete signed conservation report.
    It also found two ordinary integration defects in recovery metadata and public policy history, both covered by tests after repair.
    The remaining signed-JSON browser artifact gap is stated rather than inferred away.
    That combination is useful research evidence, but it is not a production-security claim.
